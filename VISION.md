# Vision: Self-Assembly Teams

## Purpose

The purpose of this project is to document observations, lessons learned, and practical experiences from operating software engineering organizations using a self-assembly model.

Much has been written about agile methodologies, Scrum, and Team of Teams organizational structures. However, much of that material focuses on prescribed processes rather than the realities of operating such systems with real people.

This project aims to document those realities.

Rather than presenting a framework or methodology, the objective is to capture practical observations about what succeeds, what fails, and what should be considered when organizations encourage individuals to organize around work rather than assigning work through traditional management structures.

The goal is not to prescribe.

The goal is to share experience.

---

# Vision

This project is a collection of essays examining self-assembling engineering organizations.

The content should combine practical experience with thoughtful analysis, focusing on the human factors that influence whether self-organization succeeds.

Rather than arguing for or against a particular methodology, the project should explore the trade-offs involved in allowing teams to evolve dynamically.

The emphasis should remain on observation rather than ideology.

---

# Core Themes

## People Are Not Interchangeable

One of the central observations is that people cannot be treated as interchangeable resources.

Every individual brings different:

* experience
* interests
* confidence
* communication styles
* learning goals
* technical strengths
* motivations

Successful self-assembly depends upon recognizing these differences rather than attempting to eliminate them.

---

## Initiative Is Unevenly Distributed

Not everyone naturally seeks out new opportunities.

Some individuals actively search for problems to solve.

Others prefer stability and clearly defined expectations.

Neither approach is inherently better.

A successful self-assembly model must accommodate both.

---

## Skills Do Not Transfer Equally

Moving between teams is not merely an administrative change.

Different domains require different knowledge.

Context switching carries real costs.

Individuals often require time to build confidence before contributing effectively.

The organization should acknowledge these realities rather than assuming immediate productivity.

---

## Psychological Safety

Self-organization requires individuals to voluntarily enter unfamiliar environments.

This depends heavily upon psychological safety.

People must feel comfortable asking questions, making mistakes, and contributing before they fully understand the domain.

Without that safety, self-assembly becomes theoretical rather than practical.

---

## Visibility Creates Opportunity

People cannot contribute to work they cannot see.

Transparent teams make self-organization possible.

Clearly communicating:

* goals
* responsibilities
* current work
* desired skills
* open opportunities

creates natural pathways for participation.

---

## Movement Is Healthy

Teams should not be viewed as permanent structures.

Movement between teams can:

* spread knowledge
* reduce organizational silos
* improve collaboration
* encourage learning
* increase resilience

The organization should treat movement as a normal part of healthy engineering culture.

---

## Stability Still Matters

While flexibility is valuable, constant movement is not.

Teams require enough stability to:

* establish trust
* build momentum
* develop shared practices
* deliver meaningful work

Healthy organizations balance adaptability with continuity.

---

# Intended Audience

The primary audience includes:

* engineering managers
* technical leads
* architects
* organizational designers
* software engineers
* agile practitioners

The project assumes readers are interested in improving collaboration rather than adopting a specific management methodology.

---

# Writing Philosophy

The articles should remain grounded in practical experience.

Whenever possible, observations should be supported by examples, scenarios, and lessons learned.

The writing should avoid:

* dogma
* absolute rules
* prescriptive frameworks

Instead, it should encourage readers to think critically about their own organizations.

---

# Structure

The project should consist of multiple short, focused essays rather than a single comprehensive article.

Each essay should explore one idea in depth.

Possible topics include:

* joining teams
* leaving teams
* ownership
* trust
* initiative
* organizational transparency
* leadership
* mentorship
* knowledge transfer
* decision making
* incentives
* communication

Together these essays should form a cohesive body of thought.

---

# Publication

The project should be published as a simple GitHub Pages site.

The implementation should emphasize:

* Markdown-first authoring
* lightweight navigation
* version control
* easy contribution
* long-term maintainability

The technology should remain secondary to the ideas being communicated.

---

# Design Philosophy

This project intentionally avoids presenting itself as a new methodology.

Instead, it should provide thoughtful observations that practitioners can adapt to their own environments.

Readers should leave with a deeper appreciation of the human factors that influence organizational design rather than a checklist of prescribed practices.

---

# Success Criteria

The project is successful if it:

* communicates practical lessons learned from self-assembling teams
* captures both the strengths and limitations of the model
* emphasizes human behaviour over process mechanics
* encourages thoughtful discussion rather than methodology adoption
* provides useful insights to engineering leaders and practitioners
* serves as an evolving collection of organizational observations

The final outcome should become a living body of work documenting the realities of self-organizing software engineering teams and contribute to a more nuanced discussion about modern organizational design.
