---
title: "People Are Not Interchangeable"
date: 2026-06-30
summary: "The impulse to treat engineers as fungible resources is understandable. The premise is false."
draft: false
---

The impulse to treat engineers as interchangeable resources is not malicious. It comes from a reasonable desire for predictability. If any engineer can fill any role, scheduling becomes simpler, risk feels manageable, and plans look more stable.

But the premise is false.

Every person arrives with a particular history. The work they have done, the problems they have encountered, the domains where they have built confidence — these are not generic attributes that transfer cleanly between contexts. They are specific to that person's journey.

This shows up practically in self-assembling teams. When people choose which work to join, they make decisions based on interest, familiarity, and confidence. An engineer who has spent three years working on distributed systems will not bring the same things to a payments problem as someone who has spent those years building mobile interfaces. Neither is better suited in the abstract. Each brings something the other cannot replicate easily or quickly.

Self-assembly does not eliminate this reality. It surfaces it.

In more traditional assignment models, the assumption of interchangeability can remain hidden. Assignments are made by managers who distribute work without revealing the underlying logic. People may not notice, or may not feel safe saying, when they are placed in domains where they are poorly suited.

When people choose their own work, the differences become visible. Some engineers consistently move toward infrastructure problems. Others gravitate toward product work. Some want to tackle unfamiliar territory. Others want to deepen existing expertise.

These patterns are not accidents, and they are not weaknesses. They reflect who each person is and what they are capable of in a given moment.

The practical implication is not that self-assembly always produces optimal matches — it does not. People sometimes overestimate their readiness for unfamiliar work. They sometimes avoid challenges they should pursue. The system needs enough structure to surface opportunities and enough safety for people to step into discomfort.

But the starting point matters. An organization that treats people as interchangeable will design self-assembly as a resourcing mechanism — a way to fill slots more efficiently. An organization that starts from the reality of difference will design it as a way to connect particular people to particular problems in ways that serve both.

That distinction shapes everything downstream.
