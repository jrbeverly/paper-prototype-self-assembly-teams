The simplest explanation for why self-assembly fails in many organizations is not cultural resistance or management skepticism. It is that people cannot join work they cannot see.

This sounds obvious. In practice, it is persistently underestimated.

Most engineering teams communicate their work internally. They know what they are doing, why it matters, and what help would look like. That knowledge lives in planning documents, team channels, and conversations among people who are already part of the team. From the outside, the work is invisible.

This is not deliberate exclusion. Teams are busy. Communication takes effort. The default is to communicate with the people who need to know today, not the people who might want to contribute in three weeks.

But that default makes self-assembly theoretical rather than practical.

For someone outside the team to decide whether their skills would be useful, they need to know what problems the team is working on, what those problems actually require, and where gaps exist. Without that, the best they can do is ask — which itself requires enough existing relationship to know who to ask and enough confidence to initiate the conversation.

The teams that self-assembly works around are the ones that communicate outward. Not exhaustively. Not with elaborate documentation. But with enough regularity and specificity that the work is legible to people who are not already part of it.

What that looks like varies. Some teams post brief notes about what they are tackling and where they are struggling. Some maintain a lightweight list of open problems. Some take the time to say clearly, in a shared space, what kinds of skills would accelerate their current work.

None of these require significant overhead. All of them require a deliberate choice to communicate beyond the team's current boundaries.

The benefit is not only that more people can contribute. Visible work also creates shared understanding of what the organization is doing and why. It reduces the sense that information is concentrated in particular groups. It builds the conditions for the kind of trust that makes self-organization possible in the first place.

Visibility is not a state that gets established once and then held. Teams that were transparent last quarter may have grown more insular as pressure increased. The work of keeping work visible is continuous.

It is also, consistently, worth it.
