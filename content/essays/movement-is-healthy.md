There is a persistent tension in engineering organizations between the desire for team stability and the value of people moving between teams.

Stability is easy to defend. Teams that stay together develop shared language, trust, and rhythm. They learn how to disagree productively. They stop needing to explain context that has already been established. That accumulated understanding reduces friction and speeds delivery.

Movement disrupts all of that.

When someone leaves a team, their particular knowledge leaves with them — at least partially. The team loses momentum while the gap is felt. The person joining a new team goes through an adjustment period that is rarely fast or comfortable. Neither the team nor the individual is as productive immediately after the transition as they were before it.

These costs are real. They are also often overestimated.

The assumption underlying excessive stability is that knowledge and relationships built within a team would not be useful elsewhere. But that is rarely true. An engineer who moves from a data platform team to a product team carries with them a particular understanding of the infrastructure the product team depends on. That knowledge does not disappear at the team boundary. It travels with the person and becomes useful in ways it could not be when the person was isolated within the platform context.

Movement spreads knowledge that would otherwise stay contained. It builds relationships across organizational lines that make future collaboration easier. It exposes people to different problems, which tends to improve judgment even when applied back to familiar domains.

There is also a less visible benefit: teams that expect movement stay more legible. When people know that someone might join the team next month, or that a current team member might leave, they invest in documentation, onboarding, and shared understanding. The work becomes less dependent on knowledge that lives in particular heads.

None of this means that constant movement is healthy. It is not. Teams need enough continuity to build the trust and momentum that makes them function well. The costs of movement are real and should be absorbed deliberately rather than minimized or ignored.

The question is not whether movement is good or bad. The question is whether the organization treats it as a normal part of engineering culture or as a problem to be managed away.

Organizations that treat movement as normal build the practices — outward communication, accessible onboarding, shared understanding — that make it work. Organizations that treat movement as exceptional tend to develop the conditions that make it costly when it inevitably happens anyway.
