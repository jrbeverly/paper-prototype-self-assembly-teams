---
title: "Initiative Is Unevenly Distributed"
date: 2026-09-10
summary: "Self-assembly tends to reward people who actively seek opportunities. That is not a design flaw exactly, but it is a limitation worth understanding."
draft: true
---

Some engineers actively scan for problems to solve. They read project channels outside their immediate work, track what neighboring teams are doing, and move toward challenges before anyone invites them. When new work opens, they are often already aware of it.

Other engineers prefer to be pointed at work. They do not seek out open problems — not from lack of ability, but from a different relationship with uncertainty. They work well, sometimes exceptionally, when they know what is expected of them. Self-directed search is not how they naturally operate.

Neither preference is a character flaw. Each describes a way of working that fits certain environments better than others.

Self-assembly tends to favor the first group.

When participation is voluntary, the people who show up are often the people who were already looking. If opportunities exist but are not actively surfaced to people who do not seek them, participation skews toward those with the highest initiative rather than those best suited to the work. The result can appear like widespread engagement when it is actually drawing from a narrower pool than the organization realizes.

One useful distinction is between opportunities that exist and opportunities that are findable. A team that posts its work in shared channels provides equal access to the information. But access to information and the inclination to act on it are different things. Someone who does not habitually scan those channels will not encounter the opportunity even if it was technically visible to them.

Consider a team working on a data reliability problem that would benefit from someone with operational experience. They post a note in a shared channel describing the challenge and the kind of help that would be useful. Engineers who happen to monitor that channel, or who were already curious about the team's work, are well positioned to respond. Engineers who were not looking may never know the opening existed.

The posting team did not do anything wrong. The limitation is structural: a system that relies entirely on individual initiative will reflect the distribution of that initiative rather than the distribution of relevant skill or interest.

Some organizations address this by pairing broadcast availability with direct outreach — not assigning people, but letting someone know that an opportunity exists and might be a good fit. Others create structures that regularly surface opportunities to people rather than requiring people to find them. Whether either approach fits depends on the organization.

What matters is recognizing the gap. If participation in self-assembly reliably draws the same people, that may reflect the opportunity structure as much as individual differences. The goal is not to make everyone operate the same way. It is to avoid building a system that only works for one of the two natural modes.
