---
title: "Skills Do Not Transfer Equally"
date: 2026-09-10
summary: "Moving between teams is rarely as simple as the calendar suggests. Domain knowledge, confidence gaps, and the costs borne by the receiving team all need to be part of the accounting."
draft: true
---

When someone moves from one team to another, the formal part of the transition is brief. A few conversations, some updated assignments, a change in which standups they attend. What takes longer — sometimes much longer — is becoming genuinely effective in the new context.

Technical skill is more context-dependent than it appears from the outside.

An engineer who has spent two years on an infrastructure team knows how that system behaves under load, which failure modes recur, where the documentation understates the complexity, and which approaches have been tried and abandoned. That knowledge is real and valuable. It is also not directly portable to a product team working on, say, a billing and invoicing system. The engineering fundamentals transfer. The domain-specific operational intuition does not.

This creates a gap between capability and effective contribution. In a familiar domain, a skilled engineer can assess problems quickly, form reasonable hypotheses, and move with confidence. In an unfamiliar one, the same engineer must build context from scratch. Until that context accumulates, their contribution rate is lower than it will eventually become — and lower than colleagues who have been there longer.

The gap is particularly visible in the early period of a move. Questions that seem basic from the outside may require asking for help, which itself requires enough trust to feel comfortable doing. This is one of the places where [psychological safety]({{< ref "psychological-safety.md" >}}) becomes relevant: engineers who feel safe asking questions rebuild context faster than those who do not.

The receiving team absorbs real costs during this period. They are doing their own work while also providing context, reviewing contributions more carefully, and answering questions that would not arise from someone already familiar with the domain. These costs are worth acknowledging even when the eventual value of the transfer is high. Organizations that treat them as negligible tend to plan timelines that underestimate how long effective integration actually takes.

There is also a less obvious cost on the sending side. The knowledge the person carried leaves with them, at least partially. The people who remain must cover what had been distributed across a larger group, and must rebuild institutional understanding that now lives elsewhere.

None of this argues against movement. The value of bringing knowledge into a new context — and the ways that people develop through unfamiliar work — can outweigh the transition costs considerably. But the accounting should include both sides.

Organizations that navigate this well tend to be realistic about transition timelines, clear about what support the receiving team will need to provide, and honest with the person moving about what they will need to build before they are fully effective. Those expectations, named explicitly, are easier to manage than the same realities left unstated.
